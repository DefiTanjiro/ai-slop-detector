# Direction

The tells fill a gap where a decision should have been. The direction is that decision,
written down before any pixel or sentence. It takes five minutes and decides whether the
work is any good.

## 1. Inventory the subject

List eight to twelve things that belong to this product and to no competitor. Look for:

- **Its name** and what the name means.
- **Its output:** what it produces. Invoices, routes, bookings, diffs, loaves, reports.
- **Its objects:** the physical things it deals with. Cards, parcels, keys, receipts, valves.
- **Its place:** a city, a street, a type of room, a landscape, a time of day.
- **Its numbers:** price, capacity, minutes, the year, a limit it lives with.
- **Its constraint:** only by appointment, one batch a month, closed in winter, on-call at night.
- **Its printed things:** a ticket, a label, a manual, a timetable, a statement.
- **Its users' rituals:** month-end close, the 9 a.m. standup, the deploy, the night shift.

The idea and the visual come from this list, not from the category.

## 2. The five lines

**Product.** What it does and for whom, in one plain sentence.

**Character.** Two or three concrete words, such as `calm, exact, a little warm` or
`loud, fast, local`. Banned: clean, modern, sleek, minimal, premium, seamless, elegant, <!-- scan:ignore -->
innovative. They describe every template.

**World.** The place or material the product belongs to. A ledger tool sits near paper,
ink and stamped dates. A node-infrastructure product sits near server rooms, uptime
boards and night shifts. A bakery sits near flour, ovens and the street it stands in.
The world tints the neutrals, picks the accent and supplies the visual.

**Moment.** One or two places where the work gets rich color, material or motion, chosen
from what the product shows: a chart where each color means a series, a rendered object
the product deals with, one photograph of its place. Everything else stays quiet.

**Signature.** One small detail only this product would have, used consistently. A block
height ticking in a node dashboard. A stamped date on an invoicing tool. A route line on a
delivery app. One detail, not five.

These examples show the shape of an answer. Derive yours from the inventory, even when an
example happens to fit.

## 3. The idea sentence (pages and posters)

> On this page, **[something from the inventory]** becomes **[the device that carries the page]**.

Examples of the shape: the product's own output becomes the hero visual; the street name
becomes the type treatment; the printed receipt becomes the pricing section.

"Clean and modern" is not an idea.

## 4. Two tests before building

**Generic test.** Ask what a similar prompt for a similar product would produce. Write that
version in one line: palette, type, hero, sections. Every part of your plan that matches it
is a default. Change it, and note what you changed and why.

**Competitor test.** Put the nearest competitor's name into your headline and idea sentence.
If both still work, they belong to the category, not to this product. Go back to the inventory.

## 5. Plan the tokens

Before code, write a compact plan:

- **Color:** four to six named values. Ground, surface, ink, secondary ink, accent, one
  semantic pair if needed. Build ramps in OKLCH when you can, so steps stay even in
  lightness and the hue does not drift.
- **Type:** one family for the interface, at most one partner (a display face for large
  headlines, or a mono for code and data). Name each role.
- **Layout:** one sentence and a rough ASCII wireframe for the first screen. Say where the
  text aligns.
- **Principle:** one line on what makes this piece belong to this product.

## 6. Existing brands

If the product has a real logo, color or typeface, keep it and build the direction around
it. A real brand color becomes the accent and leaves the surfaces. A violet-to-blue
gradient, a sparkle or a glow is not a brand just because it sits in the logo, unless the
user says it is.

## 7. Writing has a direction too

For a post, article, cover letter, README or product doc, write four lines first:

- **Reader:** who reads it and what they already know.
- **Job:** the one thing they should know, decide or do after reading.
- **Proof:** the real facts, numbers and examples you have. Nothing outside this list gets
  stated as fact.
- **Voice:** two or three words for the register, matched to the author and the channel.

## 8. Show it, then wait

In Fix mode and for any large Create task, show the direction (and the audit, if there is
one) and wait for the user's answer. Changing the direction later means redoing every pass.
Skip the wait only when the user already said to proceed.
